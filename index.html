Rules for this block

Easy means easy. Keep easy runs under 151bpm and never let them drift over 156. If you can't hold a sentence you're stealing from Saturday.

No intervals, no threshold sessions. You don't need speed to finish 100km. Every extra kilometre of easy aerobic running is worth more than a fast Tuesday and costs you less.

Run the downhills deliberately on every long run from 19 September. Quad damage is what ends 100k races and downhill running is the only thing that trains for it.

Getting sick again ends this race. You lost two weeks to a cough and you're about to spend four days shaking hands at an expo. Protecting your immune system now matters more than any single session.

Drop the Wednesday or Friday session before you drop a long run. If something hurts for three runs in a row, stop and tell me.

Long run, week by week

Weekly volume

What this block is, honestly

You get one peak weekend, not three. 38km on Saturday 26 September then 16km on the Sunday. That 54km back-to-back is the session that gets you to Whakaipo Bay. Everything before it is preparation and everything after it is taper.

Two weeks of the build happen at 35,000 feet and on hotel treadmills. So the trip stops being a build and becomes maintenance: hold the frequency, hold the aerobic fitness, come home healthy. You still run six days a week over there, just shorter. The real work happens in the 30 days after you land.

The bank is not empty. On 1 August you ran 22km with 503m of climbing on the Friday night and a 42km marathon in 3:53 the next morning, even splits, closing the last kilometre at 5:17. That back-to-back is already in your legs and it's a better 100k signal than the marathon time on its own.

What the disruption costs you is time, not the finish. The cutoff is midnight, 18 and a half hours. Off this block I'd put you at 14 to 16 hours with sensible pacing. Chasing 13 from here is how you blow up at 60km.

The biggest risk is not fitness. It's arriving home on 16 September sick or injured. Sixty hours in aircraft cabins, four days presenting at an expo, dinners most nights. Look after yourself over there and the last month takes care of itself.

1 to 16 September

Maintenance

You still train every day over there, just smaller. Six runs a week, mostly 6 to 10km, plus two treadmill incline sessions. Frequency is what holds fitness on a trip like this: six short runs beat one heroic session and five blank days, every time.

Two sessions actually matter. Sunday 6 September in Amsterdam, your one free morning, is a real 22km long run. And the Dubai treadmill blocks, where incline at walking pace is the closest thing you'll get to 1,853m of climbing. Hotel gyms are genuinely good for that and almost nobody uses them properly.

You are allowed a life. Drinks in Amsterdam, four expo dinners. Just know each night out costs you the next morning's run and a day of recovery. Pick two and be honest about which.

Day by day

Flights

    Staying healthy

      Treadmill sessions that are worth doing

      The incline hike. 10 to 15% at 5 to 6km/h, walking hard, 3 to 5 minutes on and 2 off. That's exactly what you'll do on every climb at Taupō, and a treadmill trains it better than flat road running does. Hold the rails only for balance, not to take weight off.

      Do not run outside in Dubai. 40 degrees gives you nothing but heat stress and a wasted day. There's no acclimatisation benefit worth having for an October race in the Central Plateau.

      Treadmills read short. Set 1% incline as your baseline for easy running so the effort matches outdoors, and don't worry if the distance looks generous.

      Coming home

      London to Auckland eastward across twelve hours is the worst direction there is. Expect three or four genuinely bad days. You land Wednesday the 16th mid-afternoon and you'll feel like you've been hit by a bus.

      Wednesday: twenty minutes outside in daylight, normal bedtime, no running. Thursday: easy 6km whenever the body allows, even if that's 4pm. Friday: easy 8km, or nothing if you slept badly.

      Saturday the 19th is your first real long run back, 24km. If you've slept properly, run all of it. If you haven't, run 16km and don't argue with yourself about it. The 26th is the session that counts, not this one.

      Eat before you're hungry, drink before you're thirsty. The first 30km is where you bank the fuel that carries you through 70 to 90, and it's exactly the stretch where everyone forgets because they feel fine.

      On course: lollies, gingernuts, chips, pretzels, peanut butter and vegemite sandwiches, fruit, water, electrolyte and cola. The last two stations drop the sandwiches and oranges, so top up properly at 76km.

      Nothing new on race day. Every gel, chew and drink gets tested on the 26 September rehearsal first.

      Drop bags

      Three bags: 29km, 50km and 76km. Pack each one for the version of you that arrives, not the one doing the packing.

        The last 48 hours

          How to run it

          Walk every climb from kilometre one. Not from kilometre 40 when your legs make the decision for you. Power-hike hard, run the flats and the runnable descents, and you'll pass people all afternoon.

          The first 30km should feel embarrassingly slow. Around 7:15/km on the runnable ground, heart rate down in the 130s and 140s. If it feels good at 20km, that's the plan working, and it's still not permission to push.

          80% of this course is single trail with 1,853m of climbing. Your head needs to expect the ground to be slow, so don't panic when an early split looks soft.

          Kinloch at 76km is where the race actually starts. Sit down, eat properly, sort your feet, pick up the second light, leave inside ten minutes. Everything after that is 24km of stubbornness.
